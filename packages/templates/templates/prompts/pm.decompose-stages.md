### Specialisation for decomposing the product into stages

Stage decomposition turns scope into an order of delivery, and every later planning step works
inside one stage, so the judgement below shapes the whole run. The step brief says what to produce
and what its rules are; this is how to make the choices that the rules leave open.

1. Choose the first stage by risk, not comfort. Read the risks and the assumptions and identify the
   riskiest one; the walking skeleton should ride a capability that exercises it, and the stage's
   goal should say which. A first stage of safe, easy work defers exactly the learning the plan
   exists to buy.
2. Make each later stage independently valuable and dependent only on earlier ones. If a stage
   exists only to integrate the earlier ones, reshape it so it delivers something a user can notice.
3. Order by dependency and priority, and where two capabilities of equal priority compete for the
   same place, decide by dependency and risk and say why. Two capabilities cannot both be first.
4. Test capability size against the stage. A capability that cannot be delivered inside one stage
   should be split by user outcome, and one that reads like a single story is a story for the
   product owner. Splitting or rewriting capabilities is not part of this step: record it as an open
   question in your handoff, with the outcome-based split you would propose.
5. Leave slack. A stage planned to full capacity will slip, so state each estimate as rough, with
   the assumption behind it, and never as a commitment. If a dependency forces a heavy first stage,
   say so rather than reordering to look balanced.
6. Capabilities marked `wont` stay out of every stage; where you know the condition that would bring
   one back, put it in your handoff so it is not lost.

Check before finishing: the first stage rides a named capability and a named risk; every stage goal
and exit criterion is something a person could observe; every stage could be released on its own;
the estimates are labelled rough and their assumptions are recorded.

Common mistakes: stage boundaries drawn around teams instead of user value; a stage that is only
"integration"; capabilities so coarse that nobody can say when they are done, accepted into a stage
anyway; a stage plan that reads as a schedule commitment.
