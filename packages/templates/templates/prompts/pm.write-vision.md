### Specialisation for writing the Vision

The Vision is the anchor every later artifact traces to, and it is built only from what discovery
recorded. Work from the discovery findings in your context, not from memory of similar products. The
step brief says what to produce and where; this is the product-management judgement to apply while
producing it.

1. Reconcile with discovery first. List every finding, user need and risk from discovery, and mark
   each as reflected in the Vision (in the problem, a target user, the value hypothesis or a
   non-goal), deferred to a later artifact with a reason, or rejected with a reason. A finding with
   no disposition is an omission, and the list is your completeness check.
2. Carry the success metrics forward as discovery recorded them, with their baselines and targets
   unchanged. Do not improve or invent a metric while writing the Vision; if a metric is unusable
   (no baseline, no way to measure), say so as a finding for the discovery owner rather than
   rewriting it.
3. Keep solutions out. A Vision lists no features or capabilities: if a finding arrives as a
   solution idea, leave it out of the Vision and mention it in your closing message for the step
   that writes the capabilities. State the value hypothesis so that evidence could disprove it, and
   name the non-goals a reader might otherwise assume are included.
4. Do not fill a gap with a guess. If a field has no source in discovery, or discovery contradicts
   itself, ask the human or report the conflict; a question you leave unanswered here becomes an
   open question that blocks the gate and, later, every story.
5. At a lower project level where a Vision already exists, revise it in place and record what
   changed, and keep the body stable so the things that cite it are not invalidated for cosmetic
   reasons.

Check before finishing: every target user and every metric traces to a discovery entry; nothing in
the Vision names a technology, a vendor or a feature; a reader who has seen no other artifact can
say what the product does, for whom, and how success will be measured.

Common mistakes: solution-shaped statements in the problem or the value hypothesis; competitor
parity added with no user need behind it; a metric quietly rewritten to be easier to hit; marketing
copy in place of concrete nouns and measurable claims.
